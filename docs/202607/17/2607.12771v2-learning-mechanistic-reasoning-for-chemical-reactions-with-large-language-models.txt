Abstract 

Reaction mechanisms consist of the step-by-step sequences of elementary reactions that explain chemical transformations. Learning the mechanism logic is therefore essential for enhancing the fundamental chemical intelligence of large language models (LLMs). The stepwise deduction of reaction mechanism aligns naturally with the reasoning paradigms of reasoning LLMs. However, current chemical LLMs primarily emphasize coarse-grained name reactions for product prediction and retrosynthesis, often leading to physical inconsistencies and hallucinations. In contrast, specialized 

1arXiv:2607.12771v2 [cs.LG] 16 Jul 2026 small-scale generative models for mechanism inference typically suffer from restricted generalization capacity across diverse chemical spaces. To overcome these limitations, we built a novel, large-scale reasoning dataset of reaction mechanisms. Furthermore, we established the FukuyamaBench, a difficult benchmark derived from Fukuyama’s Advanced Organic Reaction Mechanism book, to rigorously evaluate model performance on hierarchical mechanism reasoning. Our fine-tuned Qwen3-30B-A3B achieves 8.3% exact pathway match on FukuyamaBench Set A, surpassing the specialized FlowER model (5.1%), demonstrating that mechanism-aware training substantially enhances chemical reasoning in language models. 

# Introduction 

The fundamental role of reaction mechanisms in organic chemistry cannot be overstated; they are the step-by-step sequences of elementary reactions that describe precisely how chemical changes occur at the molecular level 1–3 . Traditionally, predicting these transformations has relied heavily on recognizing coarse-grained reaction types, also known as “name reactions”, which act as convenient but superficial labels 4. While this label-dependent approach is common in current chemical Large Language Models (LLMs) for product prediction and retrosynthesis, it inherently lacks true chemical insight. By prioritizing overall transformations over fundamental reactivity, these models frequently struggle with out-of-domain cases, leading to physical inconsistencies and chemical hallucinations 5. Conversely, specialized small-scale generative models designed for mechanism inference typically suffer from restricted generalizability across diverse chemical spaces. We argue that achieving authentic chemical intelligence requires AI systems to ground their reasoning in intrinsic chemical reactivity rather than relying on memorized, label-like name reactions. This mechanistic perspective is particularly important for laboratory synthesis, where chemically plausible reasoning is necessary to handle nuanced and context-sensitive reaction scenarios. The step-by-step deduction of a reaction mechanism is intrinsically a Markov-like process, 2where each intermediate state dictates the next logical elementary step based on localized electron flow and steric environments. This sequential logic aligns naturally with the Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning paradigms of modern reasoning LLMs. Compared to small generation models, LLMs offer distinct advantages for this task. First, they allow for the natural injection of complex reaction conditions—such as temperature, light condition, equivalent weight and catalysts—directly into the reasoning context, enabling the prediction of condition-responsive reactions (fig. 1a). Second, reasoning LLMs possess the contextual capacity to map out divergent pathways, predicting major products while simultaneously rationalizing the formation of minor byproducts in reactions with multi-products. Most importantly, an LLM equipped with mechanism-aware reasoning can successfully navigate complex reactions that extend far beyond simple name reactions, significantly enhancing its generalizability to out-of-domain chemical spaces (fig. 1b). However, constructing high-quality mechanistic training data poses a unique challenge: the annotator must have access to the ground-truth reaction pathway to ensure chemical correctness, yet the resulting reasoning must read as forward prediction so the student model learns to deduce—not merely recall— each step. Based on these insights, the main contributions of this article lie in the following three aspects: 

• Establishment of FukuyamaBench: To rigorously evaluate hierarchical mechanism reasoning, we established FukuyamaBench, derived from Fukuyama’s renowned work-book on organic reaction mechanisms. Spanning real-world problems from graduate exams to cutting-edge research, this multi-tiered benchmark comprises 1,997 intermedi-ate steps across diverse fundamental reaction types. Evaluations on FukuyamaBench demonstrates that mechanism-aware post-training effectively transforms LLMs from simple pattern-matchers towards potential chemical reasoners.

# Related Work 

LLMs for Chemistry 

LLMs have been extensively explored in chemistry, covering molecular understanding, reaction reasoning, and scientific knowledge grounding. Early efforts primarily focused on prompt engineering, demonstrating that general-purpose LLMs can solve chemistry tasks through carefully designed prompts without parameter updates 7,8 . Building upon these observations, a series of chemistry-specific fine-tuned models have been proposed, including ChemLLM 9,ChemDFM 10 , and BatGPT-Chem 11 , which adapt large pretrained backbones to domain-specific corpora and tasks. To overcome the limitations of text-only representations, multimodal LLMs for chem-istry have incorporated structured molecular modalities, such as SMILES, graphs, and 3D geometries. Representative models include nach0 12 , InstructMol 13 , Chem3DLLM 14 , and ChemVLM 15 , enabling joint reasoning over language and molecular structures. More recently, reasoning-oriented chemical LLMs have emerged, leveraging reinforcement learning or struc-tured supervision to enhance multi-step chemical reasoning and interpretability, exemplified by ether0 16 , ChemDFM-R 17 , and Chem-R 18 .Despite these advances, fine-grained reasoning over reaction pathways remains largely unexplored from an organic chemistry perspective. Most existing approaches focus on the reaction-level predictions, whereas the step-wise structural transformations and mechanistic pathways that are central to organic chemistry remain insufficiently modeled. 

Generative Models in Organic Reaction Mechanisms 

Beyond reaction-level prediction, several works have explored generative modeling of organic reaction mechanisms and step-wise molecular transformations. Sequence-based approaches 5such as T5Chem 19 and PMechRP 20 model reaction pathways using text or symbolic repre-sentations, demonstrating the feasibility of learning mechanistic knowledge from annotated data. In parallel, structure-aware models explicitly operate on molecular graphs or electron flows. Graph2SMILES (G2S) 21 formulates mechanisms as sequences of graph edits, while FlowER 22 introduces electron flow matching to generate elementary reaction steps in a physically motivated manner. More recently, benchmarks such as ChemCoTBench 23 and oMeBench 24 have been proposed to evaluate step-wise chemical reasoning, with an emphasis on intermediates and pathway consistency. Despite these advances, current datasets and models often underemphasize the fundamental role of reaction mechanisms in organic chemistry, including understanding how reactions proceed, predicting major products, and rationalizing byproducts and selectivity observed in experiments.

To address these limitations, we introduced FukuyamaBench, a comprehensive organic reaction mechanism benchmark based on the Advanced Organic Reaction Mechanism work-book 25 , a classic collection of organic chemistry problems curated by the Society of Synthetic Organic Chemistry, Japan. This book consists of 3 question sets: A, B, C and an answer section. Set A contains fundamental and crucial reaction questions, while set B includes 7questions at the graduate level or of slightly higher difficulty, and set C comprises questions collected from researchers in real labs. Set A and Set B are also regarded as classic textbooks for the Chemistry Olympiad. 

Construction . First, the problem sets were decomposed into two image categories: question-reactions and answer-mechanisms. For the reaction images, RxnIM was employed to extract reactants, conditions and products. The extracted outputs were subsequently reviewed and manually corrected by domain experts to generate clean reaction texts. These reaction texts along with the mechanism image set, were then input into Gemini-3-Pro ,and the obtained mechanism texts were validated by domain experts. Subsequently, the verified mechanism texts were combined with predefined checkpoint rules and processed through Gemini-3-Pro once more. Experts corrected and confirmed the results for reaction intermediates, ensuring the step-wise atomic and electronic conservation by RDKit. Finally, the reaction equations were submitted to RxnMapper 26 , and the corresponding mapped RXN SMILES were verified by experts through generated pictures. The overview workflow is shown in fig. 2a. 

Multi-tasks . Given a reaction mechanism trajectory T , it consists of a sequence of intermediate–condition pairs {(x0 | c0), (x1 | c0), . . . , (xi | c0), (xi | c1), . . . , (xn | cm)} ⊆ T. ,where xp stands for intermediate and cq represents the corresponding reaction condition. We then defined three tasks: Step-wise Only Prediction , Pathway-only Prediction , and Pathway & Product Prediction :

• Step-wise Only Prediction : given an intermediate–condition pair ( xj | ck), the objective is to infer the subsequent intermediate ( xj+1 | ck). 

• Pathway-only Prediction : given the initial intermediate–condition pair ( x0 | c0), the sequence of conditions c0, . . . , c m, and the final intermediate–condition pair ( xn | cm), the task is to infer the intermediates x1, . . . , x n−1.

• Pathway & Product Prediction : given the initial intermediate–condition pair 8(x0 | c0) and the sequence of conditions c0, . . . , c m, the task is to jointly infer the remaining intermediates and products x1, . . . , x n.

Coarse-grained Checkpoint . Two main challenges arise in evaluating on Fukuyam-aBench: (i) Some problems in FukuyamaBench are coarse-grained. Scientists often overlook obvious or repetitive steps when writing the mechanism. (ii) Unlike the step-wise generative model FlowER, LLMs tend to generate coarse-grained steps. Thus, the alignment method proposed by oMeS is not suitable for FukuyamaBench, since oMe-Gold is a fine-grained dataset. Here, we established a rule-based method for generating intermediate checkpoints: (i) change in heavy atom connectivity or bond topology. Any product resulting from steps involving the formation or breakage of C-C, C-N, C-O or C-X bonds must be regarded as a checkpoint; (ii) transformation of functional groups. When a molecule changes from one type of compound to another, its representative structure should be recorded; (iii) Exclusion of trivial proton transfers. In the absence of changes in the acid-base conditions, consecutive proton gain and loss steps should be combined into the same checkpoint. 

Conservation . In each step of the mechanism, the reactants and products satisfy the laws of atomic conservation and electron conservation. 

Mapping . The reactants and products within each step of the mechanism ensure that the backbone atoms correspond accordingly. The numbers of the atoms at which the reaction occurs can be clearly seen. 

Training Dataset Construction 

The training data is constructed from four primary sources (fig. 3): (1) FlowER 22 , a large-scale database of 257K elementary reaction steps; (2) oMe-Silver 24 and PMech 20 , which together provide 3,039 reaction trajectories with explicit conditions (reagents, solvents, temperature); (3) The Art of Writing Reasonable Organic Reaction Mechanisms 3, from which 50 complex mechanisms are manually extracted; and (4) Gemini-synthesized reactions for targeted coverage of underrepresented reaction families. 9Figure 3: The overall training pipeline 

Quality filtering. FlowER’s raw 257K reactions are predominantly large pharmaceutical molecules (median 42 heavy atoms, 4.6 rings), reflecting its origins in industrial synthesis databases. For such large substrates, the reaction mechanisms tend to be excessively long and repetitive, making it difficult for the model to learn the underlying mechanistic principles. We therefore apply quality-driven filtering to focus on cleaner, more interpretable reactions: we retain reactions with moderate molecular size, 3–10 elementary steps, valid SMILES, and no duplicates. This yields 2,353 high-quality FlowER samples that emphasize core organic reaction types (nucleophilic substitution, elimination, addition, rearrangement) where electron flow and competing pathways can be clearly analyzed. Combined with 1,297 condition-annotated external samples, this produces 3,650 SFT training examples. For RL, we use 2,000 reactions (1,098 external + 902 FlowER) filtered by similar criteria as the on-policy training set. 

Reaction type augmentation. FlowER, being derived from patent and pharmaceu-tical literature, has limited coverage of several reaction families that are central to organic chemistry textbooks, notably pericyclic reactions (Diels-Alder, electrocyclization, sigmatropic 10 rearrangements), which constitute <0.1% of FlowER but are fundamental to any compre-hensive organic chemistry curriculum. To broaden the training distribution, we prompt 

Gemini-3.0-Pro to generate atom-mapped mechanisms for 18 pericyclic/radical families (882 reactions) and 16 named reaction families (124 reactions, including Wittig, Beckmann, Suzuki, Swern, Fischer indole, etc.). Each generated mechanism follows an explicit textbook template to ensure chemical accuracy, and all SMILES are validated with RDKit. To avoid data contamination, we never use any benchmark problem as a seed—instead, Gemini creates novel substrates from prompted reaction types. All training data is fully disjoint from the evaluation benchmark. 

CoT annotation. A key design challenge is that the annotator (expert model) has access to the full ground-truth trajectory—starting reactants, all intermediates, and final products—yet must produce reasoning that reads as forward prediction , as if the answer were unknown. This is essential because the student model at inference time sees only the starting reactants. To achieve this, we adopt a teacher–student decoupling strategy. The annotator prompt (teacher) receives the complete reaction trajectory as context and is instructed to write in predictive language (“the nucleophile will attack”, “the intermediate is expected to 

rearrange”) while explicitly forbidding hindsight phrases (“as shown in the product”, “the result confirms”). The annotator copies product SMILES exactly from the ground truth, ensuring chemical correctness, but frames the reasoning as a forward deduction. During 

conversion to training data , the ground-truth trajectory is stripped from the user prompt, so the student model only sees starting reactants and conditions—never the answer it must predict. Several annotation design choices further improve data quality: (i) Atom ID audit : the annotator must verify that all atom map IDs referenced in the reasoning actually exist in the current step’s reactant SMILES, preventing hallucinated atom references; (ii) Adaptive granularity : obvious steps (e.g., proton transfer to a strong base) receive concise 1–2 11 sentence explanations, while decision points involving selectivity, competing pathways, or rearrangements receive detailed multi-sentence analysis; (iii) Structured output : each step produces a JSON object with reaction type, reactive sites (nucleophile, electrophile, leaving group), and product SMILES, enabling automated validation. All annotations are validated with RDKit for SMILES correctness, and malformed entries are filtered before training. 

Training Pipeline 

Supervised Fine-tuning. We perform fine-tuning of Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct on 3,650 scope-matched samples for 3 epochs. Each training example follows a structured four-phase reasoning template: (A) Mechanism Analysis —analyze electron flow and identify bond changes; (B) Atom State Definition —explicitly define charge and valence of reaction centers before generating SMILES; (C) SMILES Syntax Translation —apply rule checks (e.g., no pentavalent carbons); (D) Predicted Intermediate —output the final SMILES. This structured reasoning enforces chemical validity at each step. Training details are in Appendix . We note that SFT alone underperforms the base model on exact pathway match. We found that the SFT model learns correct chemistry—its checkpoint accuracy is high (59%)—but tends to over-generate intermediate steps, making exact pathway match harder. This is consistent with prior findings that distilling reasoning traces from large teacher models into smaller ones produces excessively verbose outputs 27 . In our setting, the reasoning annotations generated by Gemini contain detailed multi-phase analysis that the student model internalizes as lengthy outputs, inflating predicted step counts beyond what exact match requires. 

Reinforcement Learning. During RL, we optimize the policy with Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) 28 , which we prefer over GRPO 29 for two reasons: (i) sequence-level grouping and clipping reduces reward variance in long-horizon mechanism prediction, and (ii) it avoids routing replay overhead, making it practical for MoE models. Hyperparameters are detailed in Appendix . We use a step-level reward design . The reward is computed at reaction-step level and 12 averaged over the full mechanism trajectory. Case Studies 

To qualitatively evaluate our model’s reasoning capabilities, we analyze two representa-tive multi-step transformations from FukuyamaBench (Figure 4). Case A057 involves a Hofmann-style rearrangement. Our model successfully navigates the complete mechanistic backbone—from N -chlorination to the critical isocyanate intermediate—whereas GPT-5 becomes derailed by overly explicit ionic bookkeeping, and Qwen3-30B-A3B fundamentally misidentifies the reaction as direct amide hydrolysis. Similarly, in Case A063, our model correctly utilizes acid protonation as an activation step to drive a productive β-elimination, yielding the target conjugated enol-ether. Conversely, GPT-5 prematurely halts at oxonium formation, treating activation as the terminal state, while Qwen3 hallucinates incorrect down-stream carbocation pathways. As detailed further in the Appendix , these cases demonstrate that our mechanism-aware training enables the model to reliably track complex chemical trajectories, successfully avoiding the superficial dead-ends and mechanistic hallucinations that plague baseline models. 

# Conclusion 

We presented a framework for teaching LLMs to reason about organic reaction mechanisms at the elementary-step level. Our contributions are threefold: (1) FukuyamaBench , the first mechanism reasoning benchmark with atom mapping, conservation validation, and multi-task evaluation across 319 problems spanning undergraduate to research difficulty, where all extracted mechanisms and checkpoints are expert-validated; (2) a distribution-matched 

16 reasoning dataset constructed from FlowER, oMe-Silver, PMech, and expert-curated sources, with structured forward-predictive annotations, validated SMILES, atom-ID audits, and prompt templates to be released for transparency; and (3) empirical validation that mechanism-aware post-training on Qwen3-30B-A3B achieves 8.3% exact pathway match on Set A, surpassing the base model (6.9%) and the specialized FlowER model (5.1%), with a Top-8 accuracy of 15.4% demonstrating strong predictive diversity. 

Limitations. A significant gap remains to frontier models such as Gemini-3.0-Pro 

(22.3%), and Sets B and C remain largely unsolved by all models, suggesting that complex rearrangements, pericyclic, and radical mechanisms require both richer training data and multi-modal capability to address. In addition, exact-match evaluation may under-credit alternative but chemically plausible pathways 32 .

Future directions include scaling training data with broader reaction type coverage, incorporating multi-modal inputs (e.g., molecular graph), extending the framework to ret-rosynthetic pathway planning, and developing agentic systems that construct intermediate reaction graphs with physically grounded, ML-potential-based feedback for pathway selection and reinforcement learning.

FukuyamaBenchmark 

FukuyamaBench was constructed from the Advanced Organic Reaction Mechanism workbook, which provides paired reaction questions and reference mechanism solutions. The original materials contain reaction schemes and hand-drawn mechanism figures rather than directly machine-readable trajectories. We therefore first decomposed the source materials into reaction images and mechanism images. For each reaction image, we extracted the reactants, products, and reaction conditions into textual molecular representations. The extracted reaction texts were then manually checked and corrected by chemistry domain experts to ensure consistency with the original problem. For the mechanism side, Gemini was used only as an assistant for extracting and formatting information from the provided mechanism figures, rather than as an unconstrained generator of benchmark ground truth. In this annotation setting, Gemini was given substantially richer information than the models evaluated on the benchmark, including the reactant and product SMILES/SMARTS, reaction conditions, and the full reference mechanism image. Based on each question as a boundary, the parsing accuracy for Set A, B, and C was 63/78, 76/131, and 34/110 respectively. Each elementary reaction step was further inspected by RDKit to ensure step-wise atomic and electronic conservation as shown in Example 1. The resulting mechanism trajectories were reviewed and corrected by domain experts. Subsequently, using the generated mechanism trajectories as inputs,intermediate checkpoints for the pathway & product prediction task and the pathway-only prediction task were derived according to predefined coarse-grained rules (See Section ). Checkpoints were also verified by experts. Finally, atom mapping was assigned using RxnMapper and verified by experts, so that backbone atom correspondence and reactive sites could be traced across elementary steps as shown in Example 2 and fig. 6. This process yielded atom-mapped trajectories with explicit intermediates, products, reaction conditions, and checkpoint-level mechanistic states. The 27 benchmark was then organized into three tasks: step-wise only prediction, pathway-only prediction, and pathway & product prediction. Importantly, during benchmark evaluation, models are given only the task-specific inputs such as reactants/intermediate states and reaction conditions; they are not given the product SMILES, SMARTS, or reference mechanism images used during dataset construction. Training Dataset 

For data construction, we first transformed raw reaction strings to a reaction-wise trajectory representation. Each reaction is stored as an ordered list of elementary steps with explicit step index, reactants, products, and raw reaction string. This preserves causal order for mechanism supervision. Identity transitions are removed during formatting, so only chemically active steps are retained. We then perform pathway-level reasoning annotation, where each sample starts from initial reactants and predicts the complete multi-step trajectory. The 30 Figure 6: Visualization of mapped RXN SMILES in B111-a. annotator is constrained to produce both free-form forward reasoning and a structured result JSON. To improve robustness at scale, we use bounded asynchronous generation, retry with exponential backoff, periodic checkpointing, and automatic resume; invalid entries, such as empty trajectories or abnormal reaction identifiers, are filtered before annotation. The valid annotations are finally converted into a standard multi-turn instruction format for supervised fine-tuning. 31 Training Details

LLMs Captures Textbook Reactivity Trends Reactivity trends and heuristics, often derived from fundamental principles and corroborated by experimental results, play a key role in teaching and understanding reactivity. These principles enable chemists to reason bottom-up, starting from basic axioms to deduce likely reaction outcomes. In contrast, current large language models (LLMs) often rely on top-down statistical learning, frequently mapping reagents to products based on memorized ”name reaction” patterns rather than physical chemistry. Therefore, a natural question arises: do the same fundamental principles emerge from models trained on large-scale chemical corpora? Do such models reflect the underlying chemical reasoning encoded in human-developed heuristics when facing complex, condition-responsive scenarios? To address this, we scrutinize the step-wise predictions of various state-of-the-art models, examining whether they align with well-established textbook principles across a spectrum of challenging chemical environments. Many organic reactions are strongly influenced by the delicate balance between basicity and nucleophilicity. As a textbook-level description, whether a reaction proceeds via proton abstraction (acting as a base) or direct substitution (acting as a nucleophile) heavily depends on the pK a values and the steric profiles of the species involved. To probe this, we designed a benchmark evaluating the competition between keto alpha-alkylation (requiring a strong base) and the SN 2 reaction pathway (requiring a good nucleophile) across a wide continuum of reagents, ranging from strong bases ( pK a ≈ 51 .0) to weak conjugate bases ( pK a ≈ − 9.0) (fig. 7a). By tracking how different models navigate this gradient, we can assess their data-driven understanding of pK a-dependent reactivity (fig. 7b). Beyond simple acid-base chemistry, true chemical intelligence requires the ability to predict condition-responsive regioselectivity and mechanism divergence. In fig. 8, we further challenge the models with classic mechanistic dilemmas: (a) regioselectivity dictated by the steric bulk of the base, such as the bulky t-BuOK favoring Hofmann elimination versus the smaller NaOEt favoring Zaitsev elimination; (b) regioselectivity driven by intrinsic ring tension; (c) 56 pH-dependent mechanistic shifts between SN 1 and SN 2 pathways; and (d) thermodynamic versus kinetic control driven by temperature gradients, such as shifts between 170 ◦C and 50 ◦C. As shown in the evaluation tables (fig. 7b and fig. 8e), small specialized generative models, such as FlowER, often exhibit rigid predictive behaviors. Because they heavily rely on the in-domain statistical distribution of their training data, they frequently fail to adapt when reaction conditions or reagent pK a values shift. In contrast, state-of-the-art LLMs—including GPT-5, Gemini-3.0-Pro, and models from the Qwen3 family—demonstrate a distinctly higher degree of generalized chemical intuition. While they may not perfectly execute these physical chemistry rules in every single instance, LLMs show a discernible, emergent capacity to recognize the impact of steric bulk, temperature gradients, and pK a thresholds. This suggests that the broad linguistic and scientific data encountered during large-scale pre-training endows LLMs with a more robust foundation for condition-responsive chemical reasoning than smaller, purely dataset-driven models.
