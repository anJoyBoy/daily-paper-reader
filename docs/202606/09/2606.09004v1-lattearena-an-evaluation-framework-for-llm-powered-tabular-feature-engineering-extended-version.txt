# LATTEArena: An Evaluation Framework for LLM-powered Tabular Feature Engineering (Extended Version) 

Ankai Hao 1, Ke Chen 1, Huan Li 1, Lidan Shou 11Zhejiang University Feature engineering remains essential for tabular data analysis, and Large Language Models (LLMs) have emerged as a promising paradigm for automating this process, giving rise to LLM-powered AuTomated Tabular feature Engineering (LATTE). However, the absence of standardized platforms prevents fair, cost-aware comparisons. Furthermore, complex methodological designs obscure the specific contributions of individual components; for example, although LFG integrates Tree-of-Thought, few-shot demonstrations, Monte Carlo Tree Search, and natural language generation, the isolated impact of each technique remains unquantified. To address these challenges, we introduce LATTEArena, the first competitive evaluation framework featuring: (1) a six-dimensional taxonomy decomposing 15 representative methods into reusable components; (2) a standardized modular arena for controlled comparison; (3) multi-dimensional assessments covering performance, cost, and robustness; and (4) component-level ablation quantifying each technique’s competitive edge. Through extensive evaluations, we reveal 16 key findings, including: (1) Tree-of-Thought with Monte Carlo Tree Search achieves optimal cost-effectiveness; (2) RPN and Code output formats dominate classification and regression tasks, respectively. We publicly release the modular framework and over 4000 execution logs, enabling researchers to seamlessly pitch new techniques against existing ones and advance LATTE.  

> Keywords: large language models, tabular data, automated feature engineering, benchmark evaluation

1 Introduction 

The “Garbage In, Garbage Out” principle underscores that data quality bounds model performance. Feature engineering (FE) [ 56 ] thus serves as a critical bridge between data and algorithms. Although deep learning has diminished its role in computer vision and natural language processing, feature engineering remains essential for tabular data, which is prevalent in recommendation systems, healthcare, and finance. In these domains, tree-based models consistently outperform deep learning in efficiency, speed, and interpretability, and their sensitivity to feature design further elevates the importance of feature engineering. Given that roughly 80% of data science effort is devoted to data cleaning and feature engineering [ 42 ], automating this process has become an urgent need, making Tabular Automated Feature Engineering (TAFE) a research hotspot in the AutoML era. Traditional TAFE methods largely relies on heuristic search [21 , 23 , 24 ], meta-learning [ 39 ], or reinforcement learning [ 7, 25 , 58 ]. However, these approaches are either computationally 1

With powerful semantic understanding and code synthesis capabilities, LLMs are ideal candidates for automating feature engineering. The introduction of CAAFE [18 ] marked a pivotal moment, leveraging Chain-of-Thought [ 48 ] with GPT-4 [2 ] to iteratively generate feature engineering code. Subsequently, various prompt-based [32, 38, 55] and RAG-enhanced [54] TAFE methods have been proposed. Despite the emergence of LLM-powered AuTomated Tabular feature Engineering (LATTE) tech-niques, the progress still lags significantly behind other LLM-driven tabular tasks such as table question answering and NL2SQL. The LATTE community currently faces several critical challenges. First, it is unclear which technical components actually drive performance . Current LATTE methods exhibit substantial heterogeneity in their technical configurations. They have successively introduced a myriad of techniques, ranging from prompting paradigms to information-retrieval methods to optimization algorithms. Because these methods are often proposed as monolithic pipelines, it is difficult to isolate which parts are helpful and which are merely unnecessary overhead. A systematic, component-level breakdown is therefore necessary to determine optimal combinations. Second, there is no standardized, cost-aware evaluation framework . Existing literature suffers from highly fragmented and inconsistent experimental setups: studies frequently evaluate on different datasets, employ varying downstream machine learning models, and utilize different base LLMs. This discrepancy severely hinders fair, head-to-head comparisons. Furthermore, most current evaluations focus on predictive accuracy, neglecting real-world metrics such as token consumption, inference latency, and method robustness. As a result, the critical trade-offs between performance gains and the practical deployment constraints of these methods remain unclear. To address these limitations, there is a compelling need for a standardized, competitive evaluation platform. Such a framework must not only assess the general efficacy of LLMs in TAFE but also facilitate comparisons of diverse technical configurations to elucidate their respective strengths and weaknesses. While extensive evaluation frameworks have propelled rapid progress in other LLM-driven tabular tasks, systematic assessment in LATTE remains conspicuously absent. To bridge 2this gap, we introduce LATTEArena and structure our work along two fundamental directions: (i) abstracting and disentangling LATTE components to enhance methodological transparency (Challenge 1), and (ii) establishing an extensible arena for fair and comprehensive evaluation (Challenge 2). Specifically, our main contributions are as follows: 1. Novel Taxonomy and Unified Abstraction (Challenge 1) : We propose a comprehensive tax-onomy that systematically decomposes complex LATTE methods into modular components, enabling a transparent understanding of individual techniques. Based on this abstraction, we compile representative approaches (Section 3) and curate the LATTEArena datasets (Section 5.1). 2. Extensible and Standardized Framework (Challenge 2) : We present the first evaluation harness designed for controlled, direct comparisons of LATTE techniques. By synthesizing existing pipelines into a standardized framework, we enable the seamless integration of new methods and facilitate the shift from prompt engineering to context engineering [37] (Section 4). 3. Multi-Dimensional Competitive Assessment (Challenge 2) : We go beyond predictive perfor-mance by incorporating overhead and robustness into the arena’s evaluation criteria (Section 5.2), providing empirical comparisons and practical insights into real-world deployment (Section 5.10). 4. Component-Level Ablation and Insights (Challenge 1) : Leveraging the modularity of our arena, we conduct rigorous controlled experiments to isolate and quantify the competitive advantage of individual components (Section 5), offering actionable insights into which design choices are genuinely effective and which are redundant (Section 6).

2.2 The LATTE Pipeline 

Recent advances in LLMs have opened new opportunities for AutoFE on tabular data. Unlike traditional AutoFE methods that focus primarily on statistical analysis, LLM-based approaches leverage the semantic understanding of features. By integrating contextual and semantic knowledge, 

LLM-powered Automated Tabular Feature Engineering (LATTE) enables more intelligent transformation design, helping to generate meaningful features and reduce redundancy. The formal definition of LATTE is provided as follows. 

Definition 2.4 (LLM-powered AuTomated Tabular feature Engineering, LATTE) . LATTE aims to automatically generate and refine feature transformations using an LLM as the optimization engine. At each iteration i = 1, . . . , S, its feature optimization module PM, implemented by an LLM M,takes as input the training data Dtrain , validation data Dvalid , previous context Ci−1, and historical feature engineering operations Φi−1, and outputs a new operation sequence ϕi with context Ci:

{ϕi, Ci} ← P M(Dtrain , Dvalid , Ci−1, Φi−1), i = 1, . . . , S, (6) where Φi−1 = {ϕ1, ϕ2, . . . , ϕi−1} accumulates all generated operations. LATTE follows the same bilevel structure as the automated feature engineering described in Equa-tion (5). In essence, the LLM iteratively proposes feature transformations guided by validation feedback, progressively improving downstream model performance. As shown in Figure 2, the LATTE pipeline consists of three main stages: 

(S1) Prompt Construction . The LATTE pipeline begins with the design of high-quality prompts that guide the LLM toward effective feature generation. Referring to the example in Figure 2, each prompt typically comprises six key components: (i) role , which defines the LLM’s 4perspective; (ii) task description , which specifies the prediction type (e.g., classification or regression), label column name, and downstream model to be optimized; (iii) metadata , which summarizes dataset and feature information, optionally including distributional statistics pre-computed from the tabular data; (iv) instances , which provide representative table rows for contextual grounding; (v) demonstrations , which are retrieved from log files and contain context–operation sequence pairs (C, ϕ), where C stores condensed historical metadata and reasoning traces to reduce storage overhead; and (vi) instructions , which typically define the expected output format and offer guidance on how feature engineering operators should be applied. 

(S2) LLM-powered Feature Engineering . This stage forms the core of the LATTE pipeline. The feature optimizer PM, composed of the LLM M and supporting tool functions, determines candidate transformations through iterative querying. For each dataset version, the algorithm selects appropriate prompts and updates based on its feature engineering strategy, which may follow greedy, evolutionary, or UCB-based search paradigms. At this stage, all modifications affect only the stored context C and operation sequence ϕ, each of which corresponds to a specific dataset version. Advanced prompting techniques such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT), self-consistency, and Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) [ 12 ] can be incorporated to enhance reasoning quality and diversity in the generated transformations. 

(S3) Post-processing . Since the LLM outputs feature transformations in textual form, the post-process module converts them into executable operations. The output ϕ may appear in natural language (NL), rule-based, or code format, guided by the input instructions and demonstrations. The parser translates ϕ into executable programs, applies the transformations to produce a new dataset and metadata, and records the resulting (C, ϕ) pairs in log files for further iterations.

3.1.1 Chain of Thought (CoT) 

CoT encourages LLMs to reason step by step by decomposing complex problems into intermediate steps, thereby improving reasoning accuracy. In LATTE, Vanilla CoT often includes phrases like “first...” [ 6 ] or “let’s think step by step” [ 47 ] in the prompts, guiding the LLM through analyses before deriving feature engineering operations. 

Operator-based CoT , introduced by SMARTFEAT [32 ], queries the LLM twice: first to select the operator, then the operands. 

CoT with Self-Consistency (SC) generates multiple reasoning paths for the same query and aggregates outcomes, demonstrating improved robustness when combined with Vanilla CoT [ 30 ]and Class-based CoT [ 17 ]. Rather than voting on the final operation sequences {ϕ∗

1 , . . . , ϕ∗

k } in traditional SC studies [ 45 ], LATTE studies produce k datasets {D 1, . . . , Dk}, train k corresponding models, and ensemble their predictions [ 17 ]. This yields improved accuracy while incurring higher costs than simply aggregating ϕ∗s. 

3.1.2 Tree of Thought (ToT) 

ToT generalizes CoT by structuring multiple reasoning paths as a tree [ 51 ]. It uses heuristic evaluators [ 44 ] or LLMs [ 51 ] to assess intermediate nodes and terminates once a solution is found. 

LFG [55 ] employs ToT in LATTE, where each node corresponds to a candidate ϕ that is evaluated by validation performance on Dval ,ϕ. Unlike logic reasoning or mathematical tasks, LATTE tasks do not have an objective termination condition. Therefore, exploration continues until the maximum tree depth or step limit is reached. 3.1.3 EvoPrompt 

EvoPrompt [16 ] evolves prompts via Genetic Algorithms [ 29 ] or Differential Evolution [ 10 ]to discover high-performing prompts, treating prompts as individuals, performance as fitness, and modifications as mutation or crossover . In LATTE, only the dynamic segment (few-shot demonstrations) is evolved, while the static template remains fixed. Demonstrations are organized into multiple populations, periodically evolving within and across groups to enhance diversity and quality. The specific demonstration forms are detailed in Section 3.5. ELLM-FT [ 13 ] and 

LLM-FE [1] abandon traditional mutation and crossover operators and instead let the LLM perform unconstrained modifications, making the approach highly dependent on the LLM’s inherent capabilities. In each round, demonstrations are selected from each population and appended to the prompts. Since each population requires one LLM query per round, this incurs high query overhead. 

3.1.4 Optimization by PROmpting (OPRO) 

OPRO [ 50 ] frames the LLM as an optimizer, iteratively refining outputs based on feedback from an objective function evaluator. The best-performing generation at each round becomes the candidate of the next round. This paradigm has demonstrated progress on classical problems such as the Traveling Salesman Problem and datasets like GSM8K. In Vanilla OPRO [ 59 ], the validation loss is leveraged as feedback. It augments CoT by generating a set of candidate features at every reasoning round and selecting the optimal one. CART-based OPRO [38 ] extends this by training a Classification and Regression Tree and replacing metadata with reasoning extracted from it. 3.1.5 Generator-critic 

This is a multi-agent paradigm that separates generation and evaluation into two interacting agents: a generator, which produces candidate outputs, and a critic, which analyzes these outputs and provides guidance for improvement. The critic’s feedback is injected into the generator’s subsequent prompt, forming an iterative closed loop that enables optimization-like behavior. In LPFG [ 14 ], this interaction is realized as a duet-play process, where the critic diagnoses the feature set and conditions the generator’s next iteration accordingly. Rouge One [ 5 ] adopts a conceptually related approach, distributing critic functionality across multiple specialized agents whose evaluations and tests inform future generations within a collaborative pipeline. 

Takeaway: In LATTE, prompting techniques are better understood as search policies than as reasoning templates. Their real difference lies in how much exploration, feedback, and overhead they introduce into the pipeline. 

3.2 Feature Engineering Strategies 

The prompting technique defines how the LLM M is queried within PM, while the feature engineering strategy determines how to optimize the resulting operation sequence ϕ based on the generated prompt output. This section analyzes five existing feature engineering strategies, each compatible with various prompting techniques. Specific examples of the strategies are shown in Figure 3. 3.2.1 Expand-Reduce (Figure2.a) 

This strategy consists of two main phases and is commonly used in classic AutoFE algorithms [ 21 –23 ]. The first step is Expansion : PM constructs a set of prompts based on the initial tabular data and associated metadata, and queries the LLM S times, yielding a set of ϕ. The second step is Reduction : A feature selection algorithm is then applied to the generated set to identify an 8appropriate subset, which is taken as the final answer ϕ∗. Figure 3.a shows an example using Forward Selection [ 59 ]. After sorting all ϕs in ascending order of loss (ϕ), they are applied to the initial dataset one by one until the loss no longer decreases. Suppose that the last applied sequence is ϕk, then ϕ∗ = ϕ1 + ϕ2 + · · · + ϕk. Due to the low time cost of the Expand phase, S can be readily scaled to large values; however, finding the best subset requires 2S times model training, which is extremely costly. Although Forward Selection reduces the number of training to S + k, as a greedy algorithm, it does not consider interactions among features, making it difficult to find the optimal subset. 

3.2.2 Greedy Incremental (Figure2.b) 

This strategy fits well with the iterative framework. In the i-th iteration, PM performs a query using Prompt i−1, which was constructed from the table and metadata of the last round, and obtains the current round’s output ϕnew . Then PM decides whether to adopt ϕnew based on its evaluation score. While this score can be derived from metrics such as mutual information or feature importance, the prevailing approach in LATTE is to use loss (ϕ) directly [ 18 , 32 , 38 ]. If loss (ϕi−1) > loss (ϕi−1 + ϕnew ), then ϕi will be updated and executed to generate a new version of the table and metadata, which will be used to update the prompt for the next iteration. Although this method considers the interactions among features, its greedy nature makes it susceptible to convergence to local optima.

3.2.4 Best-of-N (Figure2.d) 

This is a simplified variant of Expand–Reduce: after the Expand phase, it simply picks the ϕtop that has the lowest loss (ϕ) as the final output. This strategy shifts PM’s burden to prompt engineering techniques and relies on M’s reasoning power, so it is typically used together with EvoPrompt to generate a set of high-quality ϕ during the expand phase [ 1 , 13 ], as shown in Figure 3.d. Moreover, because the simplified Reduce phase lowers the number of downstream model training times and thus cuts overhead, this method can generate a fairly large candidate set during the expand stage. 93.2.5 Select-Expand-Ensemble (Figure2.e) 

This is another Expand–Reduce variant that splits LATTE into three phases. It begins by selecting multiple data subsets from D based on label and/or feature relevance. The standard expand phase then generates FE operation sequences for each subset. Some works also incorporate a Reduce phase, for example, FREEFORM [30 ] uses LLM scoring to perform feature selection. Finally, the locally optimal sequence is applied to each subset, and the transformed tables are merged into a single dataset. Notably, existing LATTE methods defer ensemble to the prediction stage: they train a downstream model on each optimized subset and then ensemble the models [17, 30]. 

Takeaway: The FE strategy operationalizes LATTE’s exploration-exploitation trade-off: greedy refinement is efficient but vulnerable to early commitment, while broader search is more robust but much more expensive. 

3.3 Metadata 

Metadata denotes descriptive information about data attributes rather than data content itself. In tabular tasks, metadata characterizes the table’s structure, provenance, and intended use, thereby facilitating improved interpretability, management, and processing. For LATTE methods that utilize metadata, we categorize them based on how the metadata is obtained or enriched. 

Native . Metadata residing inherently within source database systems or extracted directly from web-based repositories. 

Human-Written . Precise attribute descriptions and rigorous data-type specifications manually curated by data engineers to ensure high-fidelity context [18]. 

LLM-Generated . Used in scenarios where native metadata is insufficient [ 46 ]. LLMs are queried to generate or augment metadata based on feature names and existing metadata. 

Calculated Value . Metadata derived from statistical measures such as mean and standard deviation. For example, the PyMFE [ 3] library computes metadata in five categories, including General, Statistical, and Information-Theoretic. These values complement native metadata by providing LLMs with information about data complexity and distribution patterns. 

RAG-Enhanced . Metadata enriched via RAG. This enables methods such as RAFG [ 54 ] and Rouge One [5] to generate interpretable features for domain-specific prediction tasks. 

Takeaway: Metadata is the main source of semantic grounding in LATTE, but more metadata is not necessarily better. The real challenge is to retain the most decision-relevant semantics while controlling token cost. 

3.4 Data Sampling Method 

In tabular tasks, the sheer volume of rows often exceeds LLM context limits, necessitating data sampling. In LATTE, these samples enable direct learning of feature-label relationships and reinforce the understanding of metadata. Existing methods can be classified into three categories based on their sampling approaches. 10 Random-Selected . Select an instance set randomly. 

Cluster-based Stratified . First introduced in FeatLLM [17 ] alongside Class-based CoT, it clusters instances by label and then selects from each cluster to capture class-specific patterns. 

Human-Selected . In domains like finance and biology, tables often exhibit high cardinality and class imbalance, making cluster-based sampling impractical. GPT-Signal [46 ] therefore employs domain experts to identify representative samples manually. 

Takeaway: In LATTE, sampling is a context-selection problem rather than a miniature version of full-data analysis. Its purpose is to expose representative feature-label patterns under strict context limits. 

3.5 Demonstration Composition 

In LATTE, the candidate demonstration pool for the k-th round is composed of the historical records

from previous rounds. The demonstrations generated in each round are generally composed of three parts: operation sequence ϕ, score computed based on loss (ϕ), and the reasoning provided by the LLM, which is included in C. Some LATTE methods, such as ELLM-FT [ 13 ], use RL-based TAFE methods to collect records in advance to obtain a richer candidate pool. LATTE then constructs the demonstration section using the candidate pool. We classify existing works into five categories based on their construction strategies. 

Ranked . Candidates are ranked in ascending order according to their scores. This strategy is frequently used in conjunction with EvoPrompt to help LLMs identify evolutionary patterns [ 1 , 13 ]. A typical ranked scheme is Top-k, where only k candidates with the highest scores are retained. This strategy both controls prompt length and encourages the LLM to utilize high-quality ϕ repeatedly. In Adda [34 ], candidates are ranked by a similarity metric defined as the product of semantic and structural similarity. Takeaway: The value of demonstrations depends less on how much history is included than on how that history is organized. The main difficulty is not adding more records, but distilling stable guidance from locally valid histories. 

3.6 Output Format 

Prior studies show that LLM reasoning is sensitive to output format constraints: increasing formal and syntactic constraints often degrade performance [ 43 ]. Therefore, existing LATTE methods adopt different output formats that trade off formal constraint, expressiveness, and executability. We categorize these formats into four types, and representative examples are shown in Figure 2. 

Natural Language (NL) . In this format, the LLM describes feature operations in free-form natural language, which supports transparent reasoning. However, the lack of structural constraints makes post-processing difficult, and NL-based methods are therefore typically limited to low-order operations per round [28, 30, 32]. 

Rule . Leveraging the interpretability of decision trees, LLMs can generate rules analogous to tree structures, where each rule corresponds to a binary feature [ 17 , 38 ]. Despite offering greater structural clarity, this format cannot represent continuous features. 

Code . LLMs generate code that directly implements FE operations, typically using Python [ 1, 18 , 34 ]. Despite its high expressiveness, code generation introduces strong syntactic and environmental constraints, making it prone to runtime failures. 

Reverse Polish Notation (RPN) . RPN encodes feature operations as postfix sequences (e.g., 

f1; f2; +; square ), enabling unambiguous execution. Despite its strict formal constraints, RPN avoids code-related failures and supports high-order operations within a single round, thereby expanding the feature exploration space and motivating its adoption in recent LATTE methods [13, 14, 59]. 

Takeaway: Output format is a first-order design choice because it determines both the reachable feature space and the failure mode. More expressive formats enable richer transformations, but also raise execution risk.

In Stage 1) Prompt Construction , the Serializer ingests task specifications, metadata, and tabular data to instantiate prompt templates based on technique configuration ( ➊). It supports diverse prompting strategies through a unified prompt template library covering techniques in Section 3.1, operators used in existing works, and output formats in Section 3.6. The Retriever queries the History Database for prior FE records and assembles demonstrations using strategies in Section 3.5 ( ➋). In Stage 2) LLM-powered FE , the FE Agent processes assembled prompts, where the optimizer PM

In Stage 3) Post-process , the Post-processor converts the FE Agent outputs into executable code and updated metadata ( ➍). Subsequently, the Feature Selector filters all features using criteria such as mutual information and feature importance, and correspondingly updates the code and metadata. While prior studies primarily focus on prompting strategies, they often overlook output format compliance, which is essential for automatic parsing and execution. We define the Success Rate as the fraction of LLM outputs that can be correctly parsed and executed without runtime errors. LATTEArena improves this metric by enforcing stricter format constraints during prompt construction and enabling LLM-based reformatting, yielding a more robust and automation-oriented pipeline. The Evaluator then scores features using downstream models referred to as validation models (➎). As discussed in Section 3, most methods use validation loss as the score. Furthermore, LATTEArena integrates NAS [ 35 ] and HPO [ 52 ] to enable realistic evaluations across diverse downstream models within an AutoML setting. The History Database archives metadata, code, and evaluation scores from each iteration ( ➏). Together with the Retriever, it implements a preliminary form of context management [ 37 ], which is largely overlooked by existing LATTE methods that primarily focus on improving the FE Agent. 13 Despite its advantages, LATTEArena suffers from a cold start problem: the initially empty History Database leaves early prompts without demonstrations. The Warm-up Module mitigates this by pre-populating the database using RL-based TAFE algorithms [ 31 , 53 ] ( ➐), enabling few-shot learning from the first iteration and accelerating convergence of the iterative pipeline. 

4.2 Revisiting Existing Methods 

Based on our taxonomy (Section 3), we decompose existing LATTE approaches into three stages along six orthogonal dimensions, each encompassing multiple technical options, as summarized in Table 1. Current LATTE methods exhibit substantial heterogeneity in their technical configura-tions, which poses significant challenges for fair comparison and obscures quantitative assessment of the contribution of individual components. Moreover, several representative methods [ 17 , 55 ]do not incorporate feature selection mechanisms, placing them at an inherent disadvantage in evaluations. With the proposed taxonomy and the LATTEArena framework, different technical combinations can be implemented via simple pipeline configuration, enabling controlled and fair comparisons across methods. However, this flexibility also exposes a large combinatorial design space when considering all possible technique choices. 

4.2.1 The Combinatorial Explosion Problem 

To evaluate whether each technique effectively assists LLMs in performing TAFE while identifying optimal LATTE configurations, a straightforward approach would be to enumerate all possible combinations exhaustively. However, naive enumeration is computationally prohibitive for com-prehensive evaluation. For example, considering only the techniques marked as special in Table 1, including 7 prompting strategies, 5 demonstration choices, 3 metadata options, 3 FE strategies, and 4 output formats, the total number of configurations already exceeds 1000. To efficiently navigate this vast design space, we introduce three Configuration Principles . These principles identify meaningful and representative configurations by systematically comparing, consolidating, and selecting among existing techniques. The quantitative experimental results validating these principles are detailed in Section 5.6: 

(1) Focusing on Core Methodologies. Selected techniques should represent methodologically distinct approaches rather than stylistic variations (e.g., minor rephrasing of prompts). We further exclude techniques dependent on external resources (e.g., human-in-the-loop, RAG/KG, pre-trained embedding models) to assess the intrinsic capabilities of standalone LATTE methods faithfully. Model ensembling strategies (e.g., Select-Expand-Ensemble) are evaluated separately under the AutoML setting rather than included in the main configuration space. 

(2) Respecting Component Constraints. Technique combinations must respect inherent design con-straints and employ validated defaults. For instance, MCTS is paired with ToT, as MCTS was proposed as an improvement over Greedy Search specifically for ToT; EvoPrompt requires Best-of-N for multi-population evolution; OPRO and EvoPrompt are paired with output formats that can represent higher-order features; and Warm-up is limited to RPN due to constraints imposed by the RL data collector. 

(3) Prioritizing Cost-Effectiveness. While advanced techniques like Least-to-Most prompting [ 57 ]excel in general reasoning tasks, their complexity introduces nontrivial token and computa-14 tional overhead in the LATTE domain. We therefore prioritize a systematic exploration of simpler configurations, as they may reveal more cost-effective solutions that deliver comparable performance. 

4.2.2 Resulting Configuration Space 

Applying these principles reduces the design space to 24 core configurations. Combined with the four metadata types evaluated in Section 5.6, this yields a total of 96 configurations. They can be mapped to existing LATTE methods, serving as proxies on the LATTEArena, as shown in Table 2. These configurations span four core dimensions: prompting technique, FE strategy, output format, and demonstration, ensuring comprehensive coverage of key design choices. The default configurations for the remaining dimensions are as follows: metadata is a combination of native and calculated values, the data sampling method is cluster-based stratified, and the operator set comprises all operators from existing methods. These configurations integrate and improve upon implementations of original methods, thus satisfying Principle 2. The CoT and ToT Families have richer configurations enabled by Principle 3.

We conduct repeated experiments for all methods, which is equivalent to combining each con-figuration with the Best-of-N strategy in LATTE. The Rule output format restricts LLMs to binary feature operators for classification on linear models, demonstrating limited model and task adaptability, and is therefore excluded from our configuration space. We leave more complex configurations to future work, as they require novel techniques beyond the scope of LATTEArena.

5.1 Dataset 

Existing LATTE research predominantly relies on classic benchmarks where SOTA tabular models already perform competitively without feature engineering, thus limiting the observable necessity and contribution of TAFE. To enable more rigorous and discriminative evaluation, we construct 16 LATTEArena using the “hardest” datasets from TabZilla [ 36 ], supplemented with datasets from an existing study [15]. The construction adheres to three guiding principles rooted in LATTE’s core motivation: (1) Datasets should present non-trivial challenges, for which feature engineering is necessary and can yield meaningful performance gains. The specific selection criteria follow TabZilla’s guidelines. Furthermore, we exclude datasets on which existing LATTE methods already achieve near-perfect performance, such as balance-scale (LLM-FE got 99%) and car (LLM-FE got 100%). (2) Raw data and metadata are retained to preserve semantic fidelity and to enable LATTE’s LLM backbone to utilize contextual information that traditional tabular models and TAFE methods typically cannot exploit. (3) Features should exhibit heterogeneity, either across data types or in their underlying nature. For example, datasets dominated by homogeneous features, such as cnae-9 [ 8] with word-frequency attributes, are excluded. This better reflects real-world TAFE application scenarios [15]. The final dataset suite spans multiple real-world domains, including healthcare, finance, and biology, covering sample sizes ranging from 294 to 1025009 instances and feature dimensions ranging from 5 to 118 features. The specific attributes of the datasets are listed in Table 3. To our knowledge, this is the first effort to define dataset selection criteria for the LATTE task. In LATTEArena, we use these carefully curated datasets to evaluate existing technique combinations, addressing the lack of comparisons across different scales and tasks [ 17 , 18 , 32 ], while filtering out simple datasets to highlight performance gaps.

5.2 Evaluation Setting 5.2.1 Metric 

To comprehensively evaluate the performance of LATTE algorithms, the benchmark incorporates a set of complementary metrics covering predictive performance, computational efficiency, and 17 robustness. 

Performance Gain is evaluated using three metrics: VG (Validation Gain), TG (Test Gain), and AG (AutoML Test Gain). VG and TG measure absolute performance improvements on the validation and test sets using a fixed downstream model, with accuracy used for classification tasks and normalized root mean square error (N-RMSE) used for regression tasks. AG reflects the absolute performance improvements on the test set that LATTE brings to the AutoML pipeline. In our experiments, this is implemented using AutoGluon [ 11 ], a widely adopted tabular AutoML framework that integrates various models, including traditional methods such as Random Forest, gradient boosting trees such as LightGBM and XGBoost, as well as deep learning models such as NeuralNetFastAI. 

Computational Efficiency consists of two parts: token efficiency = Performance Gain Token Cost and time efficiency = Performance Gain Time Cost . In LATTEArena, the time cost primarily stems from LLM inference and evaluator training. The inference time is positively correlated with token cost, while the training time is affected by factors such as the downstream model size and computing hardware. Therefore, we focus more on the token cost. 

Robustness plays a critical role in LLM-driven data science research; however, it remains underex-plored in most existing LATTE methods. To fill this gap, we perform repeated runs of each method on each dataset and compare their Success Rates. 

5.2.2 LLM 

In this study, we select multiple LLMs for comparative experiments, covering both open-source and closed-source models, as well as thinking and non-thinking models. These include GPT-4o ,

> Deepseek-V3.1

, o4-mini , and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct . The default LLM is GPT-4o , with temperature set to 1. 

5.2.3 Parameter Settings 

LATTEArena employs a 3:1:1 split for training, validation, and testing, with experiments con-ducted over 6 random splits. The ToT family utilizes a binary tree with 5 exploration steps, while other families perform 10 iterations. The Evo family divides demonstrations into 5 populations. 

Autofeat runs for 2 steps. The default value for top-k is 3, and the default downstream model is RandomForest. Following ELLM-FT , the warm-up module uses GRFG and adopts its settings to collect 450 demonstrations. OPRO has 10 optimization steps in each iteration.

With small validation sets, the LLM outputs become overly tailored to specific data distributions and models.

5.5 Token Efficiency Analysis (RQ 1.3) 

Previous studies have lacked a systematic comparison of different LATTE methods under the same token budget, limiting practical guidance for real-world applications. From Figure 5 and Table 4, we observe that low-cost CoT methods have already achieved leading performance on regression tasks, but the cost-effectiveness of different methods on classification tasks is still difficult to compare intuitively. Therefore, in this section, we select 9 methods and plot VG curves against token consumption in Figure 6. Methods are selected based on their cost-effectiveness under fixed-round settings. Datasets are selected to exhibit large VG variance across methods, which facilitates comparison analysis. 

Finding 8. CoT methods excel in very-low-budget scenarios, while ToT methods outperform across a wider budget range. Our analysis reveals that CoT methods exhibit impressive initial VG growth but quickly reach local optima due to their greedy strategies. In contrast, ToT methods, though slightly underperforming compared to the best CoT variants at low budgets, consistently surpass them in high-budget scenarios by balancing exploration and exploitation. 

Finding 9. Complex methods show limited cost-effectiveness despite potential performance gains. Critic, OPRO, and Evo family methods exhibit relatively low token efficiency and are likely to demonstrate advantages only when cost constraints are relaxed, particularly for EBR w, which significantly lags in the early stages. 

Finding 10. Demonstrations yield marginal gains that seldom justify the additional overhead. Although Positive-Negative demonstrations may enhance performance gain in fixed-round settings, these slight improvements fail to translate into superior token efficiency. To further investigate the cost-effectiveness of different LATTE configurations, we tested CoT methods under higher token budget scales (140k+ tokens) and compared their cost-effectiveness with OGR , the best-performing high-cost method under fixed-round settings. The results are shown in Figure 7. We observe that simple CoT methods, even those without demonstrations, consistently outperform OGR .

Finding 11. OPRO iteratively optimizes the quality of a single output, which is less cost-effective than multiple independent LLM queries.

Unlike CoT, some ToT variants exhibit an inverse time-token relationship driven by MCTS dynamics. As shown in Figure 5, the ToT family displays patterns that diverge from CoT, particularly in time cost trends. Statistics for these exceptional variants in Table 5 reveal that MCTS-induced variability in LLM query counts drives this inverse relationship: (1) When an optimal path is apparent, MCTS focuses on exploitation and visits fewer nodes. Providing demonstrations enhances the model’s ability to identify promising paths early, resulting in fewer queries with lower total time, despite higher per-query token consumption. (2) When high-value nodes appear at varying depths, the NL format hinders their discovery. The RPN format, by enabling the generation of high-order features, brings high-value nodes to shallower tree levels, allowing MCTS to discover more nodes with comparable UCB values and thereby promoting exploration. 5.9 Robustness Analysis (RQ 3.2) 

This section examines the robustness of LATTE methods across datasets and random splits using success rate as the evaluation metric, as methods with higher success rates exhibit more stable performance. Results are presented in Figure 9. 

Finding 15. Success rates decline noticeably as the FE output format becomes more complex. As illustrated in Figure 9, a consistent trend emerges: RPN underperforms NL, while Code exhibits the lowest success rates. We observe that the flexibility of the Code format frequently induces problematic LLM behaviors, such as referencing non-existent operators or features, or even manipulating prediction targets to inflate performance metrics. These issues result in a substantial number of runtime errors. 

Finding 16. OPRO significantly enhances robustness. OGR and OGC c achieve the highest success rate of 99%, attributed to their adoption of OPRO. In each iteration, these methods generate and refine just one new feature expression, thereby maintaining a focused and streamlined optimization process. 

5.10 Recommendations 

In this section, we summarize the current experimental findings and provide recommendations on the optimal technical configuration for deploying LATTE in real-world applications. 

(1) Considering all metrics, the optimal configurations among existing LATTE methods are ToT and MCTS-based methods with NL or RPN output format ( TMR and TMN ), which achieve strong performance, best token efficiency, and high robustness. 

(2) With sufficient token budget, combining OPRO with the Best-of-N strategy is recommended to get optimal performance gain. 

(3) The Code format performs better for regression tasks, whereas the RPN format excels in classification tasks. 26 (4) When budgets are extremely tight, we recommend using RPN-based zero-shot methods such as CGR and TMR .

(5) To optimize cost, we recommend simplifying metadata and instances and incorporating manual or low-overhead error-correction mechanisms to improve success rates. 

6 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

In this paper, we introduced LATTEArena, a comprehensive benchmark with a novel taxonomy and standardized evaluation framework for LLM-powered automated tabular feature engineering. Through systematic evaluation, we identified key findings: (1) No single LATTE method consistently dominates; (2) Complexification of methods fails to bring higher cost-effectiveness; (3) Existing demonstration forms remain cost-ineffective. These findings indicate that current bottlenecks lie in the retrieval, processing, and management of contextual information, suggesting a paradigm shift from prompt engineering to context engineering. Promising future directions include advanced context engineering (better retrieval and history management), richer scoring formats beyond VG, more sophisticated FE Agent strategies, and SFT/RL paradigms tailored for LATTE. We hope LATTEArena will serve as a solid foundation for future research.
