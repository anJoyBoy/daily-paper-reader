Abstract —Frequent Itemset Mining (FIM) is a foundational task in data analytics, but its candidate and conditional pattern spaces can grow rapidly, and maintaining support information becomes increasingly costly on dense datasets. These bottlenecks present a critical opportunity for quantum computing to re-design the way candidate representation and support verification are organized. Motivated by recent developments in quantum computing, we propose the QuantumFreqMine (QFM) frame-work for FIM. QFM introduces three mechanisms: (1) Bit-Vector Qubit Encoding , which stores each itemset as an M -bit transaction-indicator bit-vector over the M transactions that contain it; (2) Mining-Aware Candidate Superposition , which maps structurally valid candidate itemsets at a given lattice level to basis states of a candidate-index register; and (3) Bit-Parallel Threshold Marking , which is the reusable support-verification oracle that loads cached bit-vectors, computes their reversible intersection, performs popcount and threshold comparison, and then uncomputes auxiliary registers. We provide a theoretical analysis in terms of query depth, gate count, qubit footprint, and the output-sensitive footprint of cache updates. We implement QFM with IBM Qiskit and validate its support-verification component on Amazon Braket. The experiments report timing results, logical resource usage, and ablation results, showing that the proposed components reduce the logical circuit depth of support verification and candidate listing. 

Index Terms —Frequent itemset mining, quantum computing, Grover search, quantum amplitude estimation, association rule mining 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Quantum computing has transitioned from early theoretical conjectures to a rapidly advancing physical reality. While the foundational concepts proposed by Feynman [1] and Deutsch [2] have remained central for decades, recent break-throughs in hardware architectures have shifted the narrative from theoretical skepticism to tangible implementation [3]– [5], an era inaugurated by Google’s 2019 quantum supremacy demonstration with its 53-qubit Sycamore processor [6]. Re-cent industry milestones further confirm a definitive leap toward scalable utility. In 2024, IBM achieved hardware throughputs exceeding hundreds of thousands of operations per second, while Microsoft successfully demonstrated reli-able, error-corrected qubits. Furthermore, Google’s late-2024 

Willow chip proves that scaling hardware actively reduces system errors, completing complex tasks in minutes that would cost classical supercomputers septillions of years. 1 The extraordinary potential of this paradigm shift stems from quantum superposition , which allows an n-qubit register to simultaneously represent a state space of 2n possibilities. 2

By amplifying the probability of desired outcomes [7], quan-tum algorithms can effectively bypass exhaustive sequential search to achieve massive parallel processing. This prop-erty provides a critical opportunity to address the intrinsic computational bottlenecks. Foundational quantum algorithms have already demonstrated this immense potential: Shor’s algorithm achieves exponential speedup for factorization [8], Grover’s algorithm provides quadratic speedup for unstruc-tured quantum search [9] and counting [10] quadratically accelerates frequency estimation. It is envisaged that these quantum capabilities can effectively improve the efficiency of traditional database and data mining, such as Frequent Itemset Mining (FIM). Since FIM inherently involves exploring an exponentially growing candidate lattice and computing support counts across massive transactions, the intrinsic advantages of quantum parallelism via superposition offer a highly promising solution to break through classical performance limits. The landscape of FIM has continued to evolve from classic itemset mining algorithms, such as Apriori, FP-Growth, and Eclat [11]–[13], toward high-utility and weighted mining [14]– [16], top-k pattern discovery [17], [18], and stream, incremen-

Despite these extensive algorithmic and architectural optimizations, existing methods are designed for classical execution models, in which candi-dates must be stored as explicit memory objects and support counts are maintained via sequential database scans or com-plex tree structures. Consequently, they inevitably suffer from severe memory bottlenecks and unmanageable computational overhead when handling massive databases or extremely low minimum support thresholds. However, quantum computing presents a unique opportunity to fundamentally bypass these limitations. Unlike classical paradigms that must explicitly instantiate every candidate as a separate memory object and iteratively scan transactions to verify support, a quantum-based approach leverages superposition to maintain vast candidate spaces simultaneously within a compact quantum register. By evaluating the entire candidate space collectively rather than sequentially, quantum computing eliminates the need for exhaustive database scans and memory allocation, thereby offering a powerful paradigm to overcome the persistent bottlenecks of classical mining algorithms. While these quantum advantages are highly promising, directly applying classical FIM algorithms to a quantum framework is impractical. Quantum computing operates un-der unique physical constraints, such as strict computational reversibility and the high cost of maintaining quantum co-herence, which introduce entirely new design bottlenecks. 3

Consequently, a reliable quantum formulation for FIM must address three connected challenges. C1) Reversible data-access mismatch. Classical FIM algorithms achieve highly efficient support counting by employing compressed tree- or prefix-based structures such as FP-tree [12], H-struct [24], and related variants (e.g., CP-Tree [25] and CanTree [26]). These structures are effective for classical sequential traversal, where the next node can be chosen by following a data-dependent pointer and immediately overwriting temporary memory. However, quantum computing requires all operations to be strictly reversible in order to prevent memory overwriting from collapsing the fragile quantum state. If data-dependent pointer traversals are applied, the quantum system cannot simply overwrite memory states. Instead, it must allocate ancillary qubits 4 to record the exact branching history for later uncomputation. This naive adaptation rapidly exhausts memory with unmanageable garbage qubits. Therefore, it is imperative to develop a state-preparation scheme that restricts the quantum superposition to valid candidates, bypassing the deep, data-dependent circuits otherwise required to encode such irregularity. In this paper, therefore, we explore quantum computing for the classic frequent itemset mining (FIM) problem [11], [27]. The proposed framework, QuantumFreqMine (QFM) ,introduces three design components to address the three challenges above. 1) To resolve C1 , we introduce Bit-Vector Qubit Encoding by structuring transaction data into branchless bit-vectors, completely bypassing the garbage-qubit overhead of data-dependent pointer traversals and strictly guaranteeing systematic uncomputation. Furthermore, we design the count-ing oracle Ocount to effectively reduce support-evaluation complexity to O(√M ) (where M is the number of transac-tions), yielding a theoretical quadratic speedup over the O(M )

bound of classical sequential scans. 2) To overcome C2 , we design Mining-Aware Candidate Superposition by mapping the sparse candidate domain onto a compact space of index pairs, strictly confining the quantum superposition to valid parent combinations rather than the full O(2 N ) lattice. This shift to parallel bit-wise computation enforces a strictly bounded O(log M ) depth, ensuring the iteratively queried oracle executes reliably within hardware coherence limits during amplitude amplification. We theoretically analyze QFM in terms of time and space complexity as well as logical resource usage, implement the framework on IBM Qiskit and Amazon Braket, and eval-uate QFM on real-world datasets against various classical baselines, including a GPU-parallel approach. Our evaluations confirm that QFM successfully maintains a strictly shallow circuit depth, demonstrating the practical potential of quantum parallelism to eventually bypass the persistent bottlenecks of classical mining algorithms. Classical candidate, vertical, and tree-based mining. Fre-quent itemset mining (FIM) has been studied through several classical design paradigms. Apriori [11] leverages downward-closure pruning for level-wise candidate reduction, Eclat [13] evaluates support via vertical tid-list intersections, and FP-growth [12] bypasses explicit candidate generation entirely by recursively mining prefix-tree compressed databases. 7 To ac-celerate these foundational paradigms, subsequent efforts have heavily optimized the underlying algorithms through advanced data representations (e.g., compact array-based variants), hash-ing mechanisms, and bitwise set operations []. Beyond stan-dard frequent itemsets, researchers have broadly extended these classical principles to alternative pattern definitions, including closed itemsets [28]–[31], maximal itemsets [32]– [34], high-utility itemsets [35], and top-k mining [17], [36]. However, despite these diverse algorithmic advancements and extended pattern definitions, the efficiency of these classical methodologies still relies on assumptions that cannot directly transfer to quantum computing. Their mechanisms do not support quantum reversibility, state preparation restricted to valid candidate itemsets, or shallow circuit construction for repeated support verification, and therefore cannot address C1 ,

C2 , and C3 in FIM. 

Distributed and GPU-accelerated mining. To handle mas-sive datasets, distributed frameworks such as Dist-Eclat [37], PFP [38], and Spark-based EAFIM [39] partition mining tasks across clusters, while GPU-oriented approaches accel-erate parallel counting or itemset operations on many-core devices [23], [40]. These studies improve the throughput of classical mining by assigning candidate generation, data partitioning, and support evaluation to multiple workers or threads. However, they accelerate candidate generation and support evaluation on classical hardware, but do not change them into quantum-compatible operations. Therefore, they also cannot reformulate data access as reversible operations, restrict quantum state preparation to valid candidate itemsets, or express repeated support verification as shallow circuits, and thus cannot directly address C1 , C2 , and C3 in FIM. 

Quantum algorithms and quantum mining. Quantum computing has been studied across a wide range of problems, from foundational models of quantum query algorithms and computational complexity [10], [41], [42], to quantum simula-tion and combinatorial optimization [43], [44], and to domain applications such as finance [45]. These studies demonstrate the broad applicability of quantum computing, but they are not designed to address FIM. Among mining-related studies, qARM [46], [47] is an early attempt at association-rule mining that uses quantum counting and search for candidate-level support estimation and threshold selection. However, it relies on a classical join-and-prune procedure outside the quantum circuit for candidate generation, lacking a complete quantum mining formulation. B. Preliminary of Quantum Computing 

In this subsection, we first describe quantum states, includ-ing qubits, basis states, measurement, and superposition, to explain how quantum information is represented and read. We then introduce quantum circuits, including gates, circuit depth, reversibility, and uncomputation, to explain how quantum computing is organized and why temporary values need to be cleaned carefully. Finally, we describe oracles and marking, which are used to express condition checking inside quantum computing. 

a) Quantum states: A qubit is the basic unit of quantum information. While a classical bit has a deterministic value of either 0 or 1, a qubit can be described by probabilities over the two outcomes 0 and 1 before it is measured. b) Quantum circuits: Quantum computing is organized as a circuit , which is a sequence of gates that change the states of qubits or registers. The circuit depth counts the number of dependent gate layers in a circuit. Gates in the same layer can be executed in parallel, while gates with data dependency must be placed in later layers. A shallower circuit is gener-ally preferable because it reduces the amount of sequential quantum computation []. A fundamental property of quantum circuits is reversibility []. Each gate requires an invertible transformation, meaning the previous state can in principle be recovered from the output state. Quantum computation must evolve states through these reversible operations rather than destructive updates; otherwise, the fragile quantum state would permanently collapse. Unlike classical computation, where a program may overwrite or discard temporary values once they are no longer needed, a quantum circuit cannot simply erase them, because this would remove information needed to reverse the computation. The standard technique is 

uncomputation [], which cleans temporary values by applying inverse operations to return the corresponding registers to their original states, so that these registers can be reused in later circuit steps without carrying irrelevant intermediate information. 

c) Oracles and marking: An oracle is a reusable re-versible circuit that checks whether a state satisfies a specified condition, such as whether a candidate itemset reaches the minimum-support threshold. Since directly measuring a su-perposition would collapse it, 9 the oracle does not enumerate desired states by measurement. Instead, it marks the states satisfying the condition, so that later amplification steps [] can make these states more likely to appear as the desired observed results. IV. A LGORITHM QFM To solve FIM, we propose QuantumFreqMine (QFM) . To address the reversible data-access mismatch (C1 ), we in-troduce Bit-Vector Qubit Encoding . Rather than navigating recursive, pointer-based structures (which forces a quantum system to retain unpredictable traversal paths), our encoding explicitly structures the transaction data as a branchless bit-vector to ensure that temporary quantum registers can be systematically uncomputed and recycled, effectively avoiding the unmanageable garbage-qubit overhead []. Furthermore, we design a counting oracle Ocount that operates on these encoded states, making 1-item support evaluation compatible with quantum amplitude estimation (QAE) [10] and reducing the required oracle calls to O(√M ). This yields a quadratic

theoretical speedup over the O(M ) sequential scans inherently required by classical algorithms [11], [12]. To address the candidate-domain encoding gap ( C2 ), we design Mining-Aware Candidate Superposition . Instead of ini-tializing a quantum state over the full O(2 N ) itemset lattice or synthesizing deep, data-dependent circuits to explicitly encode a sparse, arbitrarily distributed candidate list, this design mathematically maps the search domain onto index pairs. An index pair references two combinable frequent itemsets discovered in the previous mining level, 10 avoiding the deep conditional logic required to hardcode such a discrete list into a quantum state. Because the mapped index pairs can be numbered as a contiguous index range (i.e., 0, 1, . . . ), the quantum system can straightforwardly prepare the superpo-sition while maintaining a highly optimized, shallow circuit depth. This design decouples candidate-domain construction from support verification by first restricting the quantum state to the mapped index pairs, so support checking is invoked only on the candidates represented by those pairs. The search space is therefore limited to the number of structurally valid parent pairs, rather than the full O(2 N ) itemset lattice. Consequently, achieving efficient state preparation via parallel Hadamard gates drastically reduces circuit complexity, preventing the threshold-marking oracle from wasting expensive resources on mathematically invalid itemsets. To mitigate the repeated reversible support-verification cost (C3 ), we develop Bit-Parallel Threshold Marking . Rather than unrolling classical sequential scans into unmanageably deep linear circuits, we specifically construct the threshold-marking oracle O≥σ as a highly parallelized reversible arithmetic block. By exploiting Bit-Vector Qubit Encoding, O≥σ turns support verification into a bit-vector counting problem, allow-ing the support count to be computed in parallel rather than accumulated through sequential scans. This architectural shift from sequential accumulation to parallel bitwise computation reduces the oracle’s circuit depth from O(M ) to O(log M ).This linear-to-logarithmic reduction is critical because the threshold oracle is iteratively queried during amplitude am-plification. Equipped with the above designs, QFM executes as an iterative mining pipeline over itemset sizes, comprising three modules: Quantum Prerun (QPr), Quantum Frequency Check-ing (QFc), and Quantum Itemset Listing (QIl). QFM begins with QPr, which takes the transaction database and the min-imum support threshold as inputs. QPr employs Ocount to evaluate 1-item supports, identifying the frequent 1-itemsets and initializing their corresponding Bit-Vector Qubit Encod-ing. After the 1-itemset stage, QFM iterates between QFc and QIl to mine progressively larger itemsets. Given the frequent itemsets discovered at the previous itemset size, QFc prepares the Mining-Aware Candidate Superposition and configures the reusable O≥σ to evaluate candidate supports and mark those satisfying the threshold.
